# Independent versions fail together

Versions written apart from each other catch much of what one version misses, yet they fail on the same inputs far more often than chance, a correlation that crossing model, harness or provider lowers in some measurements and leaves standing in others.

- Knight and Leveson, IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering 12(1), 1986: 27 versions of one specification, written by students at two universities and each run on one million random inputs, failed on the same inputs more often than independence predicts, so a reliability estimate for several versions has to count dependent errors.
- N-Version Programming with Coding Agents, 2026 preprint: the same experiment with five agent harnesses, 23 models and three languages, 48 of 69 versions admitted after a 200-case acceptance test, triples voted by majority; mean failures fell from 387.44 for a single version to 130.99 for a triple, while across all 48 versions the inputs on which two or more failed together numbered 429 against 115.36 expected under independence, many of which the authors trace to where the specification is hard or ambiguous. Crossing agent harnesses lowered the correlation without removing it: 87 of the 158 cross-harness pairs with a defined correlation sat at exactly 1, while 50 sat at or below 0, against 6 of 52 same-harness pairs. The paper does not split the pairs by model or provider.
- Correlated Errors in Large Language Models, ICML 2025: over 350 models on two leaderboards and a resume-screening task; on one leaderboard dataset two models agree on average about 60 percent of the time when both err, and larger, more accurate models correlate more, across architectures and providers.
- When Does a Second Model Help?, 2026 preprint: 30 Korean-language artifacts with 150 planted errors, produced by Claude Opus 4.6 per the run logs and reviewed by the producer's model and by models from two other providers. Two reviews of one artifact by one reviewer model overlapped 52 to 62 percent by Jaccard against 37 to 46 percent for two different models, which the authors call descriptive, since unequal detection rates also lower overlap. Two reviews of each artifact, one fresh same-model and one by the top-tier cross-model reviewer, found 56.7 percent of the planted errors against 42.7 percent for two fresh same-model reviews, signed-rank p=.006, on the first run; the authors note that two different reviewers raise combined recall mechanically, and that this pair was not significantly ahead of two top-tier cross-model reviews, so a different model and a stronger one are not separated. The paper reports no cost per review.
- Inferred, measured by none of these: two plans for one fix, written apart, are two versions at the stage where a design is cheapest to change, and the gain above comes from their differences, which a reviewer of either plan alone does not see. A bug often has more than one correct fix, so a difference between two plans is a choice rather than a defect. None of these measures what a second plan costs against a review round, or what a second provider adds to a plan rather than a review.

Source: [Knight and Leveson](https://libraopen.lib.virginia.edu/public_view/jd472w463), [N-Version Programming with Coding Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.20158), [Correlated Errors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07962), [When Does a Second Model Help?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01471).
